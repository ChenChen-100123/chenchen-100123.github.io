Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

PAPS and the 98% Handover Claim: Where the Evidence Stops

A single paper's numbers rarely matter outside its own subfield. What travels is the structure underneath them — and on that test the PAPS claim is worth more than the system it describes, because the same failure mode recurs wherever performance is self-measured.

PAPS, a cross-domain authentication scheme for UAV-assisted vehicular networks, combines four mechanisms: consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse. Its reported figures are striking — up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead. The problem it targets is genuine: a vehicle at highway speed crosses several MEC domains in seconds, and every boundary crossing triggers a full re-authentication, costing both time and UAV battery.

The independent evidence is less flattering. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUF implementations and found reliability falling from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — roughly 4.78% bit error, a rate that compounds across successive handovers. Until fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead and ECC design are disclosed, that result cannot be reconciled with a ~98% handover success figure. Nor does a second independent study of PUF stability under vehicular or UAV conditions appear to exist; Bian et al. stands alone. The UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% were computed leaves its cryptographic primitives and security strength undefined, so the denominator of both percentages is unknown, and neither the 156-byte overhead nor the 85%-under-loss figure has any independent replication.

This is where the case stops being local, and where its actual conclusion sits. The same structure recurs across engineering: distributed consensus benchmarks, battery-degradation models, network monitoring telemetry. In each, favourable figures taken under nominal conditions degrade once realistic operating stress is applied, and in each the mechanism is identical — self-reported evaluations omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade the result, producing a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability. The nominal case is rarely a fabrication; it is a best case presented as a typical one. That generalisation, not any single percentage, is the finding worth carrying away from PAPS.

Assessment then follows as two verification actions rather than a judgement call. First, retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories, auditing baseline fairness, parameter disclosure and worst-case operating boundaries — here, the fuzzy-extractor parameters, the UAVA configuration, and the loss model behind the 85% figure. Second, query Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress. Both are required: the first establishes what was claimed and on what terms, the second whether anyone outside the originating group has reproduced it.

A claim that survives neither is not yet a result. Neither is one that survives only the first, however clean its primary documentation — which is why the 98% handover figure fails as an engineering result and not merely as a public-relations overreach, and why any headline number offered without both checks inherits the same defect, whatever domain it comes from.
