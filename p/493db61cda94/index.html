Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

PAPS and the 98% Handover Claim: Where the Evidence Stops

The reason to examine PAPS is not PAPS. A ~98% handover success rate concerns only the engineers building authentication for UAV-assisted vehicular networks; the failure mode it exposes concerns anyone deciding whether a favourable performance number is a result or a sales figure. The finding this case supports is structural: performance measured by its own authors under nominal conditions gets quoted as typical, and it holds only until realistic operating stress is applied. That structure recurs across distributed consensus benchmarks, battery-degradation models and network monitoring telemetry. The two verification actions closing this piece are what the finding is for.

PAPS combines consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported figures: up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication. The problem is real — a vehicle at highway speed crosses several MEC domains in seconds, and each crossing forces full re-authentication, costing time and UAV battery.

The independent evidence is less flattering. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUF implementations and found reliability falling from 98.97% nominal to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — roughly 4.78% bit error, compounding across successive handovers. Until fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead and ECC design are disclosed, that cannot be reconciled with ~98% handover success. No second independent study of PUF stability under vehicular or UAV conditions appears to exist. And the UAVA baseline behind the 83.3% and 33.9% leaves its cryptographic primitives and security strength undefined, so both percentages have an unknown denominator; neither the 156-byte overhead nor the 85%-under-loss figure has any independent replication.

That is the local failure. The general one is what it demonstrates: self-reported evaluations omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade the result, so a systematic gap opens between promotional figures and production reliability — in distributed consensus benchmarks, in battery-degradation models, in network monitoring telemetry, and here. The nominal case is rarely fabrication; it is a best case presented as a typical one. That generalisation, not any single percentage, is the conclusion this case establishes.

Which makes the response procedural rather than a judgement call: two verification actions, both required. First, retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories, auditing baseline fairness, parameter disclosure and worst-case operating boundaries — here the fuzzy-extractor parameters, the UAVA configuration and the loss model behind the 85% figure. Second, query Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites to find at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports testing performance under realistic field stress.

A claim that survives neither is not a result. Neither is one that survives only the first, however clean its primary documentation — which is why the 98% handover figure fails as an engineering result and not merely as public-relations overreach, and why any headline number offered without both checks inherits the same defect, in whatever domain it appears.
