Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

PAPS and the 98% Handover Claim: Where the Evidence Stops

A single authentication scheme's handover rate would be a narrow thing to argue about. What makes PAPS worth arguing about is that its numbers fail in a way that is not specific to PAPS — and the test that catches the failure applies to any performance figure, in any field.

The claim. PAPS is a cross-domain authentication scheme for UAV-assisted vehicular networks, combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported results: up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication. The problem is real: a vehicle at highway speed crosses several MEC domains in seconds, and each crossing forces full re-authentication, costing time and UAV battery.

The friction. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUF implementations and found reliability falling from 98.97% nominal to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — roughly 4.78% bit error, compounding across successive handovers. Until fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead and ECC design are disclosed, that cannot be reconciled with ~98% handover success. No second independent study of PUF stability under vehicular or UAV conditions appears to exist. The UAVA baseline behind the 83.3% and 33.9% leaves its cryptographic primitives and security strength undefined, so both percentages have an unknown denominator, and neither the 156-byte overhead nor the 85%-under-loss figure has any independent replication.

The conclusion. This is one instance of a pattern with a stable shape — call it the nominal-case gap: self-reported evaluations omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade the result, so a systematic gap opens between promotional figures and production reliability. The nominal case is rarely fabrication; it is a best case presented as a typical one. The same shape recurs in distributed consensus benchmarks, in battery-degradation models, in network monitoring telemetry — different fields, the same omission, the same gap. That pattern, not any single percentage, is what the case establishes.

The two verification actions. Assessing such a claim is procedural, not a judgement call, and needs both steps. The first is an audit of primary archival documentation or source repositories — baseline fairness, parameter disclosure, worst-case operating boundaries: here the fuzzy-extractor parameters, the UAVA configuration and the loss model behind the 85% figure. The second is a search of Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites for at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports testing performance under realistic field stress. The first establishes what was claimed and on what terms; the second, whether anyone outside the originating group reproduced it.

A claim that survives neither is not a result. Neither is one that survives only the first, however clean its primary documentation — which is why the ~98% handover figure fails as an engineering result and not merely as public-relations overreach, and why any headline number offered without both checks inherits the same defect, whatever domain it comes from.
