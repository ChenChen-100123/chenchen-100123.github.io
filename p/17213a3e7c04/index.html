Yifan Yang

Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

Step back and the gap stops being about PAPS. Bian et al.'s 4.78% is not an anomaly; it is what nominal-to-stress degradation looks like when anyone actually measures it, and the same mechanism recurs wherever performance is self-reported. Consensus benchmarks timed on well-provisioned clusters lose throughput under partition and clock skew; battery-degradation models fitted on controlled cells drift under real thermal load; telemetry counts what the collector sees and never what the queue drops. In each case the evaluation quietly omits the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to make results worse, so the headline figure separates from field reliability slowly enough that nobody notices. What deserves scrutiny is the class of claim, not one handover percentage — and an undefined baseline like UAVA does more damage than any disputed number, because every ratio resting on it becomes uninterpretable.

Sorting one claim from another takes two moves. First, audit the primary record — the archival paper or source repository — for baseline fairness, parameter disclosure and worst-case operating boundaries, asking whether the comparison ran the same primitives at the same security strength. Second, step outside it: Google Scholar, standards repositories and independent benchmark suites, for at least two peer-reviewed studies or replication reports measuring the claim under realistic field stress. An author's own evaluation is a hypothesis; independent reproduction under stress is a result.
