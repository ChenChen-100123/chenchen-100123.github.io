Yifan Yang

Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

The gap is not PAPS's alone. Bian et al.'s 4.78% is not an outlier but the ordinary shape of degradation: a number measured at nominal conditions, quietly re-measured by reality. The same shape recurs wherever performance is self-reported — consensus benchmarks tuned on well-provisioned clusters lose throughput under partition and clock skew, battery-degradation models fitted to controlled cells drift under real thermal load, telemetry counts what the collector sees and never what the queue drops. The shared mechanism is omission: the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to make results worse are exactly the ones left out. That is why the undefined UAVA baseline does more damage than any single disputed figure — every ratio resting on it, 83.3% and 33.9% included, becomes uninterpretable, and the class of claim, not the handover percentage, is what warrants scrutiny.

Two checks separate a claim from a result. Audit the primary record — archival paper or source repository — for baseline fairness, parameter disclosure and worst-case operating boundaries, asking whether the comparison ran the same primitives at the same security strength. Then step outside it, to Google Scholar, standards repositories and independent benchmark suites, for at least two peer-reviewed studies or replication reports measuring the claim under realistic field stress rather than simulation. An author's own evaluation is a hypothesis; independent reproduction under stress is a result — and until that exists, 98% and 156 bytes remain promises, not findings.
