The PAPS Evidence Gap in UAV-Assisted Vehicular Authentication

Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a hard operational problem. Vehicles moving at highway speed cross multiple MEC domains in seconds, and each boundary can trigger a full re-authentication, consuming time and UAV energy. PAPS proposes to avoid that cost by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported results include up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

The strongest external check comes from Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023), who measured 16 FPGA PUF implementations. Reliability fell from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress, equivalent to ≈4.78% bit error. In a handover protocol that reuses PUF-derived identity, that error can compound across repeated cross-domain switches. This does not by itself refute PAPS, but it creates a specific evidentiary burden: unless fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead and error-correction design are disclosed, a ~98% handover success rate is difficult to reconcile with the measured PUF instability.

The replication gap is wider still. No second independent study was found that evaluates PUF stability in vehicular or UAV settings. The UAVA baseline is undefined, and neither the 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead nor the 85%-under-loss figure has independent replication. Baseline ambiguity is not a minor citation issue; it determines whether an improvement is real or relative to an artificially weak comparator. Worst-case operating boundaries matter for the same reason: field stress, not nominal conditions, is where authentication either holds or fails.

The lesson extends well past one authentication scheme. This evidence gap is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains — from distributed consensus benchmarks to battery-degradation models to network monitoring telemetry — a recurring pattern emerges: favorable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

That is why the PAPS case matters beyond a single-scheme dispute: it is a compact test of how performance claims in stressed, safety-adjacent systems are assessed. From this pattern, rigorous assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture resolves into two verification actions: first, retrieving and inspecting primary archival documentation or source repositories to audit baseline fairness, parameter disclosures and worst-case operating boundaries; and second, querying Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress.
