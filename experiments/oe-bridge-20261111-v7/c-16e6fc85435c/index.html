# Structural-Review Rule: Separate "Review Was Done" from "Component Was Covered"

This page sets out a reusable rule for reviewing future structural changes and load-sensitive decisions, and explains the bridge case that gives the rule its point. The rule is a review method: apply it to the decision in front of you, and keep only the short memory entry below for later sessions. The full page is context, not a record to save.

## The core distinction

Two statements can both be true and still differ in importance:

- A standard review process was completed.
- A particular safety-critical component was actually included in the underlying capacity or design calculation.

The first does not establish the second. The central move in this rule is to check coverage directly rather than inferring it from the completion of a familiar process.

## The rule

**Step 1 — Name the material dependencies.** Before relying on any conclusion, list the components and assumptions on which that conclusion materially depends. Treat as material anything that, if wrong or missing, could change the answer.

**Step 2 — Verify coverage.** For each safety-critical item on that list, confirm that it was included in the relevant capacity or design calculation, and within that calculation's stated scope and assumptions. Do not treat a checked-off review, a completed checklist, or a summary as evidence of coverage on its own.

**Step 3 — Pursue unsupported assumptions.** If a critical assumption is unsupported, inconsistent with the records, or outside the existing assessment, go to the relevant design records. Where the records leave the question open, seek an independent technical assessment.

**Step 4 — Stop at sufficiency.** Close the review once the material assumptions are adequately supported for the decision at hand and no unresolved evidence gap could reasonably change the conclusion. Continuing past that point adds cost without adding confidence.

## Worked sequence: the I-35W case

Engineering procedures provide a foundation for technical decisions, but their conclusions rest on assumptions about design, loading, and component capacity. On August 1, 2007, the I-35W bridge over the Mississippi River in Minneapolis collapsed. According to the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), the investigation identified inadequate gusset-plate capacity arising from a design error, together with deficiencies in the relevant calculations and design review. Later changes to the bridge and the construction loads present at the time also formed part of the circumstances.

Read through the rule's steps, the case illustrates why the distinction in the opening section matters: confirming that a familiar review process was performed is not the same as confirming that a particular safety-critical component was covered by the underlying calculation. The rule is a method for making that check explicit. It does not replace case-specific engineering analysis or independent professional judgment.

Source: [NTSB I-35W investigation](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/HWY07MH024.aspx).

## Simulated supporting records (R-17)

The records below are **simulated** for this exercise. They are illustrations only and do not describe the real I-35W investigation or any real project.

- **R-17a — Review checklist (simulated).** A generic design-review checklist was marked complete, but its scope statement did not name gusset-plate capacity among the items checked.
- **R-17b — Calculation note (simulated).** A capacity calculation covered primary members but left the gusset-plate connection outside its stated assumptions.
- **R-17c — Follow-up query log (simulated).** A reviewer asked for the original connection design records; the reply was a summary that did not resolve whether the connection had been covered.

Together the three simulated records trace the rule in order: name the material component (Step 1), test whether the calculation actually covered it rather than trusting the completed checklist (Step 2), pursue the design records when coverage stays unclear (Step 3), and stop only when no unresolved gap could change the conclusion (Step 4).
