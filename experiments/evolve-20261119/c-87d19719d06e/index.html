PAPS and the Evidence Gap in Cross-Domain Authentication By Yifan Yang

In UAV-assisted vehicular networks, cross-domain authentication remains a critical unsolved problem. A vehicle travelling at highway speed crosses multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each boundary crossing currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV energy. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported results include up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

Independent evidence complicates that picture. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023) measured 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and found reliability falling from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage/temperature stress — an ≈4.78% bit error rate that compounds across handovers. Because PUF-based identity is load-bearing in PAPS, this is not a peripheral caveat: it bears directly on whether the reported ~98% handover success can hold outside controlled conditions. Similarly, the UAVA baseline is undefined, so the claimed gains lack a clear comparator. The 156-byte overhead and the 85%-under-loss figure also have no independent replication.

The stakes are practical rather than academic. Procurement decisions, certification filings, and safety cases all turn on figures of this kind, and a favorable number is not a finding until its baseline, operating envelope, and replication record are visible. Absent those, a headline gain can migrate from a paper into a specification and then into a deployment where the environmental assumptions no longer hold. The asymmetry is structural: the party reporting a percentage also selects the conditions under which it is measured, which is why the decisive evidence has to come from measurement that the reporting party does not control.

That pattern is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains — from distributed consensus benchmarks to battery-degradation models to network monitoring telemetry — favorable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

Rigorous assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture therefore resolves into two concrete verification actions: first, retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories to audit baseline fairness, parameter disclosures, and worst-case operating boundaries; second, query Google Scholar, official standards repositories, or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress. Applied to PAPS, those two actions decide whether ~98% handover success is a measured property of the scheme or an artifact of the conditions under which it was reported.
