Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

When UAV Authentication Claims Meet Field Stress

PAPS, a UAV-assisted Internet of Vehicles authentication scheme, addresses a real mobility problem: a fast-moving vehicle may cross several MEC domains in seconds, and each boundary crossing normally triggers full re-authentication, consuming time and UAV energy. PAPS combines consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its authors report up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication.

Those figures are promising but not independently established. The central caution comes from Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023), who measured 16 FPGA PUFs degrading from 98.97% nominal reliability to 95.22% under worst-case voltage/temperature (≈4.78% bit error), an effect that compounds across handovers. That result does not test PAPS directly, but it undercuts any assumption that hardware-rooted identity remains stable across every operating boundary. The UAVA baseline is undefined, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have no independent replication. Without a defined baseline and replicated field-stress results, the reported gains cannot be treated as production-grade guarantees.

The issue is not whether PAPS works in a favourable demonstration. It is whether the claimed reductions survive realistic voltage, temperature, mobility and loss conditions—and whether the comparison baseline is fair. A 156-byte re-authentication figure is only meaningful alongside the message flow, security assumptions and failure cases that produced it. Likewise, ~98% handover success leaves a failure rate that matters in safety-relevant vehicle networks, where retries and fallback authentication can erase nominal savings.

This evidence gap is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains, favourable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

From this pattern, rigorous assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture resolves into two concrete verification actions: first, retrieving and inspecting primary archival documentation or source repositories to audit baseline fairness, parameter disclosures, and worst-case operating boundaries; and second, querying Google Scholar, official standards repositories, or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress.
