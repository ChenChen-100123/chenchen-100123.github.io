Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

Auditing PAPS: Nominal Gains, Field Stress and the Evidence Gap

In UAV-assisted vehicular networks, high-speed vehicles can cross several MEC domains within seconds. Each domain boundary currently forces a full re-authentication, consuming time and UAV energy. PAPS addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. The scheme's reported gains are substantial: up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication. Those are self-reported figures, and their evidential status matters as much as their magnitude.

Independent evidence shows why that distinction is consequential. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUFs degrading from 98.97% nominal reliability to 95.22% under worst-case voltage/temperature (≈4.78% bit error, compounding across handovers). That result does not test PAPS directly, but it establishes a relevant failure mode: hardware-rooted identity can lose reliability precisely where field conditions depart from nominal assumptions. In the PAPS comparison, the UAVA baseline is undefined, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have no independent replication. The comparison therefore cannot yet be audited for baseline fairness, parameter disclosure or worst-case boundaries, and the headline numbers should not be read as production reliability.

The evidence gap is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains — from distributed consensus benchmarks to battery-degradation models to network monitoring telemetry — a recurring pattern emerges: favorable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

Two verification actions follow: an audit of primary archival documentation or source repositories for baseline fairness, parameter disclosures and worst-case operating boundaries; and a search of Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites for at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress.
