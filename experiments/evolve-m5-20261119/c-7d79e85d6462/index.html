Cross-domain authentication in UAV-assisted vehicular networks is a critical unsolved challenge because vehicles moving at highway speeds traverse multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each domain boundary currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes both time and UAV battery. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map lightweight cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse — reporting up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

However, independent empirical evidence introduces tension with these self-reported figures. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES, 2023) evaluated 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and measured reliability declining from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress — a 4.78% bit-error rate that compounds across successive handovers and cannot be reconciled with 98% switching success without disclosing fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead, and ECC design. No second independent study evaluating PUF stability in vehicular or UAV contexts was located. Meanwhile, the UAVA baseline against which the 83.3% and 33.9% figures are calculated remains undefined in cryptographic primitives and security strength, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have zero independent replication.

PAPS's 156-Byte Handover Claim Meets the PUF Reliability Record

The numbers are precise. The evidence behind them is not—and the reusable result is the cross-domain pattern that explains the gap plus the two checks that expose it.

In UAV-assisted vehicular networks, a speeding vehicle crosses several MEC domains within seconds, and every domain boundary forces a full re-authentication—latency spent, UAV energy spent, at each hop. PAPS combines consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported results are striking: up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication.

The Hardware Root

The hardware root is the weak link. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUF implementations and watched reliability fall from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage and temperature stress—roughly 4.78% bit error. Errors of that magnitude compound across successive handovers. Reconciling them with ~98% handover success would take more than an assertion: it would take fuzzy-extractor parameters, helper-data overhead and error-correcting-code design. None are disclosed. The UAVA baseline is undefined, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have no independent replication.

The Generalisation

The conclusion the PAPS dispute supports is broader than the scheme. It is not that PAPS is unusually weak. It is that PAPS is ordinary. Across engineering domains—distributed consensus benchmarks, battery-degradation models, network monitoring telemetry—the same pattern recurs: favourable figures reported under nominal conditions degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The mechanism is consistent. Self-reported evaluations routinely omit exactly the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, opening a systematic gap between promotional numbers and production reliability. The durable finding is therefore not one optimistic handover claim. It is that the shape of the omission is predictable, and therefore checkable before adoption rather than after deployment. The percentage table is the least portable part of the case; the generalisation and the checks are the part that travels.

Two Verification Actions

Assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture therefore resolves into two concrete verification actions. The first is to retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories, auditing baseline fairness, parameter disclosure and worst-case operating boundaries—the step that would have surfaced PAPS's missing worst-case and fuzzy-extractor parameters. The second is to query Google Scholar, official standards repositories or independent benchmark suites for at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress—the step that would have flagged the absence of independent replication for the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures. A claim that survives both is worth citing in design work; one that survives neither remains a press release, however precise its percentages—and both checks apply to the next 156-byte promise, in whatever domain it appears.
