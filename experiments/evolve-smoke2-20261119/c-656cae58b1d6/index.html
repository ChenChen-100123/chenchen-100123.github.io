Cross-Domain Authentication in UAV-Assisted Vehicular Networks: PAPS and the Limits of Self-Reported Gains

At highway speed, a vehicle can cross several multi-access edge computing (MEC) domains in seconds. Each crossing can force a full re-authentication, consuming time and UAV energy. PAPS, a proposed scheme for UAV-assisted cross-domain authentication, combines consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography and smart-contract state reuse. Its authors self-report up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

Those figures are consequential because cross-domain authentication sits on an operational boundary: a delayed or failed handover can interrupt connectivity precisely when a vehicle moves between coverage zones, while UAV energy accounting affects endurance and coverage. But the evidence base is uneven, and the headline gains should not be mistaken for established properties of deployed systems.

The most direct independent check concerns hardware roots of trust. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023) measured 16 FPGA PUFs degrading from 98.97% nominal reliability to 95.22% under worst-case voltage/temperature (≈4.78% bit error, compounding across handovers). That result does not test PAPS, but it sets a reliability burden for any PUF-based identity claim: nominal lab reliability is not worst-case field reliability. Other gaps are closer to PAPS itself. The UAVA baseline is undefined, and the 156-byte and 85%-under-loss figures have no independent replication. The appropriate conclusion is not that PAPS fails, but that its strongest numbers remain self-reported and need field-stress comparison.

This evidence gap is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains — from distributed consensus benchmarks to battery-degradation models to network monitoring telemetry — a recurring pattern emerges: favorable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

From this pattern, rigorous assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture resolves into two concrete verification actions: first, retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories to audit baseline fairness, parameter disclosures, and worst-case operating boundaries; and second, query Google Scholar, official standards repositories, or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress.
