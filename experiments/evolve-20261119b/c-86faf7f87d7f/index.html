PAPS and the Evidence Gap in Cross-Domain Authentication

In UAV-assisted vehicular networks, cross-domain authentication remains a critical unsolved problem. A vehicle traveling at highway speed crosses multiple MEC domains within seconds, and each boundary crossing currently triggers a full re-authentication that consumes time and UAV energy. The PAPS scheme addresses this by combining consortium-blockchain trust sharing, PUF-based hardware identity, Chebyshev chaotic-map cryptography, and smart-contract state reuse. Its self-reported results include up to 83.3% computation reduction, 33.9% UAV energy saving, ~98% handover success, and 156-byte cross-domain re-authentication overhead.

Independent evidence complicates that picture. Bian et al. (ACM TODAES 2023) measured 16 FPGA-based PUF implementations and found reliability falling from 98.97% under nominal conditions to 95.22% under worst-case voltage/temperature stress—an ≈4.78% bit error rate that compounds across handovers. Because PUF-based identity is load-bearing in PAPS, this is not a peripheral caveat: it bears directly on whether the reported ~98% handover success can hold outside controlled conditions. Similarly, the UAVA baseline is undefined, so the claimed gains lack a clear comparator. The 156-byte overhead and the 85%-under-loss figure also have no independent replication.

Those gaps matter for procurement, certification, and safety cases, where a favorable number is not a finding until its baseline, operating envelope, and replication record are visible.

This evidence gap is not unique to PAPS. Across engineering domains—from distributed consensus benchmarks to battery-degradation models to network monitoring telemetry—favorable performance figures reported under nominal conditions frequently degrade once tested under realistic operating stress. The common mechanism is that self-reported evaluations routinely omit the environmental and baseline assumptions most likely to degrade results, creating a systematic gap between promotional figures and production reliability.

From this pattern, rigorous assessment of any emerging technical claim or system architecture resolves into two concrete verification actions: first, retrieve and inspect primary archival documentation or source repositories to audit baseline fairness, parameter disclosures, and worst-case operating boundaries; second, query Google Scholar, official standards repositories, or independent benchmark suites to locate at least two peer-reviewed empirical studies or replication reports evaluating performance under realistic field stress.
